# Offensiveness scale for the Fluenough adult deck: research synthesis

The evidence points to a 4-band scale. Each band should say how a native speaker hears the word. Slurs, region and context should be separate fields on the card. No published norms exist for the app's Indian languages, so each language needs its own native-speaker rating round. No research was found for the two similarity matrices.

Only claims that passed the citation checks are used below. Where a check corrected a claim, the corrected wording is used. No primary PDF could be opened in full. Most confidence ratings are therefore Medium, based on abstracts and search excerpts.

## 2. What matters for this app

### Learners hear these words differently from natives

- **Confirmed (High).** In Dewaele (2004), "the perceived emotional force of S-T words is highest in the L1 and gradually lower in languages learned subsequently".
- Classroom learners rated force lower than people who learned the language naturalistically (Medium).
- **Nuance (Medium).** In Dewaele (2016), LX (non-native) users of English rated most of 30 words as *more* offensive than L1 users did. They underrated only the single most offensive word. They were less sure of meanings. Dewaele links the overrating to a "red flag" that classroom warnings attach to these words.
- Dewaele, J.-M. (2016). Thirty shades of offensiveness. *Journal of Pragmatics* 94, 112-127. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pragma.2016.01.009
- **Consequence.** Learners err in both directions: they overrate mid-level words and underrate the top one. The level on a card must state how a native speaker hears the word, not how strong it feels to the learner. The top band needs its own explicit warning.
- **Also (Medium).** In Jay & Janschewitz (2008), the offensiveness ratings of non-native speakers depended on their experience with English. Jay, T., & Janschewitz, K. (2008). The pragmatics of swearing. *Journal of Politeness Research* 4(2), 267-288. https://doi.org/10.1515/JPLR.2008.013

### Regional and social variation

- **Across countries (Medium).** Sulpizio et al. (2024) report "a significant amount of cross-country variability in words' tabooness and offensiveness". One check found that *maricón* is similarly taboo in Spain and Chile but more offensive in Spain, and that *puto* is more offensive in Chile. A second check could not verify this (Low to Medium). Spanish cards need a region field.
- **Context (Medium).** Jay & Janschewitz (2008): the appropriateness of swearing depends on the speaker-listener relationship, the social-physical context and the particular word. Ofcom found rating words in isolation difficult. The BBFC raises the level for aggressive or directed delivery. MPA rules treat expletive and literal sexual use differently.
- **Slurs move separately from swearing (Medium).** Ofcom 2021: audiences are more relaxed about most swearing, but concern about discriminatory language is rising. In 2020, 1% of complaints were about swearing and 8% about racial discrimination. Most of the 2016 "strongest" tier was discriminatory terms (per press summaries).
- **Indian specifics (Medium to Low).** CBFC and BCCC both single out double-meaning language. A BCCC advisory on SC/ST (caste) language is reported only by a weak, undated source. Caste slurs are a class with no UK equivalent and must be covered.
- **Content pattern (Medium).** In Sulpizio et al. (2024), the most taboo and offensive words were almost exclusively about female genitalia or female family members. This matches common Hindi and Bengali abuse, but that comparison is my inference (Low).
- **Gender (Medium).** In Dewaele (2004), women rated emotional force higher; which languages this covers is unconfirmed. Kapoor (2016) found women rated swearing less appropriate. In Beers Fägersten (2007), the gender difference was not significant (p = 0.0707).
- **Small core vocabulary (Medium).** In Jay (2009), 10 words account for 80% of public swearing in US recordings. The counts from 1986, 1997 and 2006 showed the set stable over 20 years. A short deck can cover most of what a learner will hear. Jay, T. (2009). *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 4(2), 153-161. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2009.01115.x

## 3. Options for the app's scale (best first)

| # | Option | Main downside | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Four bands, mild / medium / strong / strongest, defined as "how a native speaker hears it in general"** (Janschewitz's tabooness question). Separate fields on each card: type (swear, slur, sexual, family insult, using HurtLex-style categories), "can be friendly among peers" (the OffendES NOE idea), and a region note. Slurs against caste, religion, ethnicity, disability or gender always go in strongest. | The bands are coarse and one level hides context. Ofcom's 2016 tiers came from a small deliberative sample (248), and Ofcom may have relabelled the tiers in 2021. | Medium |
| 2 | Same as option 1, but with BBFC/Australian wording (mild / moderate / strong / very strong). | Same downsides as option 1. "Very strong" sounds less absolute than "strongest", which matters because learners underrate exactly the top word (Dewaele 2016). | Medium |
| 3 | Three bands, mild / moderate / severe (as in Kapoor 2016). | No top "never use" tier separate from strong words. Ofcom found some words "problematic for some even post-watershed". | Medium |
| 4 | A numeric score (1-9 or 0-1) from a rating study, shown as is. | Too fine to read on a card. It needs many raters per language, and no Indic norms exist to start from. Better kept as internal data behind option 1. | Medium |
| 5 | Two levels (ESRB style) or binary (FCC, CBFC, HASOC). | Too coarse to stop learners misjudging mid-level words. | High |

### How a reviewer per language would assign levels (Low: inference from the methods above)

1. Draft a candidate list from attested sources:
   - Hindi, Bengali and the other Indian languages: HASOC, MACD, BD-SHS and MOLD data.
   - Spanish: SHARE (licence permitting) and the Sulpizio et al. (2024) Spain and Chile items.
2. Have several native speakers per language rate each word, ideally from more than one region. Ask Janschewitz's question: "How offensive is this word to people in general?" Use a 1-9 or 5-point Likert scale. This follows the Revised HurtLex method.
3. Map the median rating to the four bands, with the cut points written down. Put slurs in strongest by rule.
4. Where raters from different regions disagree by a band or more, add a region note instead of averaging. For Spanish, check against the Sulpizio et al. Spain and Chile data.
5. Add a one-line context note (who says it to whom, friendly vs hostile). Record reviewer and date on the card.

## 4. Dropped claims and gaps

### Dropped or changed after checking

- Dewaele (2004): the anchor labels "does not feel strong ... very strong" are unconfirmed and dropped.
- Dewaele (2016): "LX users avoid the most offensive words" is not in the abstract. It actually says LX users reported more use of less offensive words, and L1 users more use of the more taboo words.
- Sulpizio et al. (2024): dropped the exact tabooness wording ("not acceptable ... in most social situations"), the claim that disability words rank among the most offensive in most languages, the co-author names Badan, Basclain and Brysbaert, and the claim that scores are standardized within each language.
- Mexican Spanish ANEW: the claim of "regional differences in arousal" is unconfirmed and dropped.
- Jay (1992), *Cursing in America*: the page range 159-194 and the chapter DOI are unconfirmed. Cite the book DOI 10.1075/z.57.
- BBFC: the "infrequent use of strong language (e.g. fuck)" and "strongest terms (e.g. cunt)" thresholds are 2002-era wording, not the 2024 guidelines.
- FCC: the phrase "personally reviling epithets" is unconfirmed (it comes from Chaplinsky, 1942). The claim that the FCC treats slurs as reviling epithets is dropped.
- Australia: F2012L01934 is the computer-games instrument, not the films one. Corrected to F2012L02541.
- CBFC: the citation "Rajya Sabha PQ 154 (23 May 1990)" was not found and is dropped.
- Cinematograph Rules 2024: the language rules for the A certificate come only from Wikipedia and are dropped.
- Ofcom 2016: the placements of cow, crap and cock rest only on press summaries, and the exact quotes about discriminatory words and context were not seen verbatim.
- Beers Fägersten (2007): the finding that context lowers offensiveness is attributed to Beers Fägersten (2012) and Mormol (2018). The gender effect was not significant.

### Gaps

- No primary full text was read. Scale anchors are unconfirmed for Janschewitz, Dewaele 2016, Sulpizio, Roest and Tontodimamma.
- The 2021 Ofcom tier labels are unresolved, and so is which South Asian terms Ofcom rates.
- There are no human-rated offensiveness norms for Hindi, Bengali, Telugu, Marathi, Kannada, Gujarati or Assamese (an absence finding, so Low). Kapoor (2016) appears to use English stimuli.
- The RAE "malsonante" and ASALE "vulg." / "desp." labels were not checked. Spain's regulator, CNMC, was not covered.
- **Similarity matrices (the user's request): no research was found** on words that sound like, or are spelled like, offensive words, in any of the app's languages. The only related idea is the CBFC and BCCC concern with double-meaning words. Both matrices will have to be built in-house. A possible method (Low): one list from phonetic distance over IPA transcriptions, one from spelling distance over native script and romanisation, both reviewed by native speakers. A separate literature search is still needed.
